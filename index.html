    Receipts

    • G and c are scaled together, so 1 grid unit is 11.8 km. Horizon sizes, orbital speeds, inspiral timing and GW frequencies line up with real values; on-screen time runs about 23× slower than real.
    • Inspiral comes from Peters’ quadrupole energy loss, for any pair of black holes or neutron stars. Remnant spin uses the Rezzolla et al. fit for aligned spins, radiated energy follows the remnant’s ISCO binding energy, and the ringdown uses the Berti fits for the l = m = 2 mode. 36 + 29 M☉ gives about 62 M☉, roughly GW150914.
    • Spin (χ) moves the innermost stable orbit using the Bardeen–Press–Teukolsky formula, drags nearby gas with a gravitomagnetic force, and gets spun up by accreted gas toward the 0.998 Thorne limit. Aligned spins merge later and radiate more.
    • Each hole’s shadow is the exact Kerr outline (Bardeen 1973) for its spin and your viewing angle. It is nearly round at moderate spin and only flattens noticeably on the approaching side as spin nears 1 and the view goes edge-on. The disk is brighter and bluer on the side moving toward you (beaming ∝ δ³). The disk is ray traced: each pixel near a hole follows its light back along a Schwarzschild geodesic to where it crosses the orbital plane, which is why the far side of the disk arcs over and under the shadow. The background sky is still bent with a flat point lens, and the tracing ignores spin.
    • Jet power scales with feeding rate × spin² (Blandford–Znajek). Each jet has a fast spine (0.92c) that is strongly beamed, inside a slower sheath (0.3c) that glows in every direction, so jets show from any angle and the one aimed at you is brighter. Knots move at the apparent speed for your viewing angle; on the near jet that can look faster than light, the superluminal-motion illusion seen in M87 and 3C 279. Jet length and width are drawn schematically.
    • Neutron stars are the one thing at true size: 12 km radius, 1 grid unit. Two of them merging make a kilonova; above about 2.3 M☉ the remnant collapses to a hole (in reality, after a short-lived hypermassive phase). A real kilonova fades over days; here it takes about 9 seconds.
    • Listen plays the GW frequency one octave up. The real 36 + 29 chirp lasts about 0.2 s; here it is stretched out. The other sounds (explosions, mergers, flares, feeding) are synthesized for effect and placed left or right by where they happen on screen; only the chirp is data.
    • A white dwarf that spirals into a neutron star gets torn into a disk. The neutron star feeds and spins up (about 0.1 M☉ takes it to millisecond periods), the usual story for millisecond pulsars; the fastest known spins 716 times a second. A neutron star that eats past about 2.3 M☉ collapses into a hole. A neutron star sinking into a giant makes a Thorne–Żytkow object.
    • A black hole rarely eats a star whole. Gas spills off through the Roche lobe and the star keeps going. A giant stays big until its envelope is gone, then its helium core is left orbiting, as a white dwarf for a red giant or a Wolf–Rayet star for a blue supergiant. A small star can be whittled down to a brown dwarf. On a stretched orbit, each close pass takes a little (like ASASSN-14ko, which flares every 115 days).
    • Black holes, neutron stars and white dwarfs all accept gas. A star feeding a white dwarf builds up a hydrogen layer that ignites as a nova every so often; each one throws most of the layer off, so the white dwarf grows slowly and can eventually pass 1.44 M☉ and explode as a Type Ia. Real novae eject about 0.00001 M☉; here the layer is scaled up so they come every few seconds.
    • Mass transfer is only stable when the star giving mass isn't much heavier than the one taking it. Past about 1.5×, the orbit shrinks as mass moves, the overflow grows, and it runs away into a common envelope: the companion spirals inside the star, and either throws the envelope off (leaving the bare core in an orbit many times tighter) or merges with it. Most merging black-hole and neutron-star pairs are thought to have gone through this.
    • Not all the stolen gas gets eaten. Near the hole, part of it is blown back out by its own light, 15–65% here and more when the hole is feeding hard, as in ultraluminous X-ray sources. The purple band on the scope tracks how fast gas is falling in.
    • A magnetar is a neutron star with a field near 10¹¹ tesla. Its giant flares here come every 15–30 seconds; real ones are decades apart. The 2004 flare of SGR 1806−20 released more energy in a fifth of a second than the Sun does in about 100,000 years.
    • Planets, moons and asteroids get their size from their mass, on a curve through Ceres, the Moon, Earth, Neptune, Saturn and Jupiter. Past about 13 Jupiter masses a body becomes a brown dwarf, and past 0.075 M☉ it ignites as a star.
    • Loose matter feels every body’s gravity and its own. It gathers into a body only where that body could hold itself together against the tide of whatever it orbits, which is outside the Roche limit (about 2.4 planet radii for matter as dense as the planet). Inside it, matter stays a ring. A moon that orbits inside the limit is pulled apart after about one of its own free-fall times.
    • Two worlds that meet slowly or head-on merge. A fast, glancing blow throws part of the smaller one off as molten debris; what stays in orbit outside the Roche limit gathers into moons. The giant-impact scene uses the standard picture: a Mars-sized Theia, about 45° off head-on, just above escape speed.
    • The Solar System scene uses each planet’s real mass, radius and orbit (J2000 elements run forward to late 2026), with everything flattened into one plane. Dwarf-planet positions are approximate. Belts are a few thousand sample bodies, not a census. The nearest stars are markers at their true distances, not simulated.
    • Sticking and clumping run on a coarse grid with a few thousand particles, far faster than the real thing: a planet here gathers in a minute, not a few million years.
    • Stars above about 8 M☉ can go supernova from the inspector. The iron core collapses, bounces at nuclear density, and the shock stalls until neutrinos reheat it; then it blows the envelope off at thousands of km/s. Stars born above about 20 M☉ leave a black hole here, the rest a neutron star with a kick of a few hundred km/s. Collisions can build stars no galaxy makes on its own: from about 140 to 260 M☉ the star ends in a pair-instability supernova that leaves nothing behind, and above about 260 M☉ it collapses straight into an intermediate-mass black hole. A real star like that would live a couple of million years first; here it ends at once. In reality the collapse takes a fraction of a second and the shock needs hours to reach a supergiant's surface; here the sequence takes about 5 seconds. Because stars here are drawn far smaller than real ones, ejecta is launched fast enough to escape their exaggerated surface gravity and then coasts away at a realistic 5,000–20,000 km/s. In a tight binary it is launched faster still, to keep the real ratio of ejecta speed to orbital speed (about 50 to 1 in nature), since binaries here are compressed to about 1,000 km.
    • White dwarfs are held up by electron pressure, which fails at 1.44 M☉ (the Chandrasekhar limit), and they shrink as they gain mass. Two carbon–oxygen white dwarfs that merge below the limit make one smaller, heavier white dwarf; two helium white dwarfs reignite as a helium-burning hot subdwarf, and a helium plus a carbon–oxygen white dwarf swell into an R Coronae Borealis giant. Past the limit, carbon ignites in a runaway and the whole star explodes as a Type Ia supernova, leaving nothing (some may instead collapse quietly into a neutron star; that outcome isn't modeled). A white dwarf that hits an ordinary star sinks into it.
    • Star types follow real classes, each with a real example. Red giants are Sun-like stars in old age and end as white dwarfs; red supergiants like Betelgeuse are massive stars in old age and are the most common supernova progenitors. A supernova's class (II-P, II, IIb, IIn, Ib/c) follows from how much hydrogen envelope the star kept.
    • Age it jumps a star to its next life stage, skipping the time in between, which the button notes. The path depends on birth mass: below about 0.8 M☉ stars outlive the universe so far; up to 8 M☉ they become red giants, puff off a planetary nebula and leave a white dwarf (mass from the initial–final mass relation); heavier ones become supergiants and end in supernovae, and the heaviest pass through luminous-blue-variable and Wolf–Rayet stages first.
    • Spaghettification: the hole pulls harder on a star's near side than its far side, stretching it along the line to the hole and squeezing it sideways. Past the tidal limit it is torn into a long thin stream; the near end is deeper in the hole's gravity, so roughly half the debris is bound and falls back while the rest escapes.
    • Sgr A*, the 4.3 million M☉ hole at the center of our galaxy, with five of the S-stars that orbit it on their measured periods and eccentricities (S2: 16 years, swinging to 120 AU at closest approach; S4714: so eccentric it passes within 13 AU). The real orbits are tilted every which way; here they share one plane. This scene runs at true scale with stars at their real sizes, and time is sped up so S2 laps the hole in about half a minute; the readout shows what a screen-second is in real time. S2's orbit really precesses by 12 arcminutes per lap, as general relativity predicts (measured by the GRAVITY team in 2020), too little to see here. Throw a star at the hole to see a galactic-center tidal disruption; time slows down automatically so the debris can be followed.
    • Click any object to inspect it. Masses and rates are live; rates are per second of simulation time, and orbital periods are given in real seconds. The dashed ring is the selected object's orbit around the barycenter it shares with its strongest partner, run one lap ahead with the same gravity the simulation uses, so orbits close to a hole visibly fail to close (precession) and paths that end in a plunge or a tidal disruption are marked. Other bodies and gravitational-wave losses are left out of the forecast, so in crowded or chaotic systems it's only the orbit for the moment.
    • Not to scale: ordinary stars are drawn thousands of times smaller than life (a white dwarf is really Earth-sized, about 500× a neutron star, but here only 1.6×), and mass transfer runs fast enough to watch.
