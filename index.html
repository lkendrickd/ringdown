Receipts

  • G and c are scaled together, so 1 grid unit is 11.8 km. Horizon sizes, orbital speeds, inspiral timing and GW frequencies line up with real values; on-screen time runs about 23× slower than real.
  • Inspiral comes from Peters’ quadrupole energy loss, for any pair of black holes or neutron stars. Remnant spin uses the Rezzolla et al. fit for aligned spins, radiated energy follows the remnant’s ISCO binding energy, and the ringdown uses the Berti fits for the l = m = 2 mode. 36 + 29 M☉ gives about 62 M☉, roughly GW150914.
  • Spin (χ) moves the innermost stable orbit using the Bardeen–Press–Teukolsky formula, drags nearby gas with a gravitomagnetic force, and gets spun up by accreted gas toward the 0.998 Thorne limit. Aligned spins merge later and radiate more.
  • Each hole’s shadow is the exact Kerr outline (Bardeen 1973) for its spin and your viewing angle. It is nearly round at moderate spin and only flattens noticeably on the approaching side as spin nears 1 and the view goes edge-on. The disk is brighter and bluer on the side moving toward you (beaming ∝ δ³). Lensing is still a flat point-lens approximation.
  • Jet power scales with feeding rate × spin² (Blandford–Znajek). Each jet has a fast spine (0.92c) that is strongly beamed, inside a slower sheath (0.3c) that glows in every direction, so jets show from any angle and the one aimed at you is brighter. Knots move at the apparent speed for your viewing angle; on the near jet that can look faster than light, the superluminal-motion illusion seen in M87 and 3C 279. Jet length and width are drawn schematically.
  • Neutron stars are the one thing at true size: 12 km radius, 1 grid unit. Two of them merging make a kilonova; above about 2.3 M☉ the remnant collapses to a hole (in reality, after a short-lived hypermassive phase). A real kilonova fades over days; here it takes about 9 seconds.
  • Listen plays the GW frequency one octave up. The real 36 + 29 chirp lasts about 0.2 s; here it is stretched out.
  • A white dwarf that spirals into a neutron star gets torn into a disk. The neutron star feeds and spins up (about 0.1 M☉ takes it to millisecond periods), the usual story for millisecond pulsars; the fastest known spins 716 times a second. A neutron star that eats past about 2.3 M☉ collapses into a hole. A neutron star sinking into a giant makes a Thorne–Żytkow object.
  • A black hole rarely eats a star whole. Gas spills off through the Roche lobe and the star keeps going. A giant stays big until its envelope is gone, then its helium core is left orbiting, as a white dwarf for a red giant or a Wolf–Rayet star for a blue supergiant. A small star can be whittled down to a brown dwarf. On a stretched orbit, each close pass takes a little (like ASASSN-14ko, which flares every 115 days).
  • Not all the stolen gas gets eaten. Near the hole, part of it is blown back out by its own light, 15–65% here and more when the hole is feeding hard, as in ultraluminous X-ray sources. The purple band on the scope tracks how fast gas is falling in.
  • Stars above about 8 M☉ can go supernova from the inspector. The iron core collapses, bounces at nuclear density, and the shock stalls until neutrinos reheat it; then it blows the envelope off at thousands of km/s. Stars born above about 20 M☉ leave a black hole here, the rest a neutron star with a kick of a few hundred km/s. Collisions can build stars no galaxy makes on its own: from about 140 to 260 M☉ the star ends in a pair-instability supernova that leaves nothing behind, and above about 260 M☉ it collapses straight into an intermediate-mass black hole. A real star like that would live a couple of million years first; here it ends at once. In reality the collapse takes a fraction of a second and the shock needs hours to reach a supergiant's surface; here the sequence takes about 5 seconds. Because stars here are drawn far smaller than real ones, ejecta is launched fast enough to escape their exaggerated surface gravity and then coasts away at a realistic 5,000–20,000 km/s. In a tight binary it is launched faster still, to keep the real ratio of ejecta speed to orbital speed (about 50 to 1 in nature), since binaries here are compressed to about 1,000 km.
  • Click any object to inspect it. Masses and rates are live; rates are per second of simulation time, and orbital periods are given in real seconds.
  • Not to scale: ordinary stars are drawn thousands of times smaller than life (a white dwarf is really Earth-sized, about 500× a neutron star, but here only 1.6×), and mass transfer runs fast enough to watch.
